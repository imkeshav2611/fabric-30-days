The problem

The old warehouse works. That is the problem.

1It runsBut the nightly load takes hours
2It is trustedBut only two people understand it
3It is paid forBut the hardware is ageing

Nobody wants to touch it, and every year it gets harder to leave.

The simple version

Moving house without losing anything.

You do not carry everything out in one trip. You list what you own, throw out what you no longer need, move room by room and check nothing is missing.

A migration is the same: inventory, convert, move, verify.

✓InventoryWhat exists, and what is used
✓Move in phasesOne subject area at a time
✓VerifyOld and new must match
How it works

The phases of a migration

1Assess: tables, procedures, jobs and who uses them
2Decide: move as-is, rewrite, or retire
3Convert: schema and code to the new platform
4Run both in parallel and compare the numbers
5Cut over area by area, then switch the old one off
Parallel running is not optionalTwo systems producing the same numbers for a few weeks is how you earn the right to switch.

The honest part

Lift and shift or rewrite?

Move as-is

  • Logic is sound and well understood
  • The deadline is tight
  • The source is close to T-SQL already

Rewrite

  • Jobs are slow by design
  • Logic is duplicated across procedures
  • Nobody trusts the current output

The catch: a large share of an old warehouse is often unused. Migrating it wastes weeks. Find out what is actually queried before converting a single table.

From real work

Migrations I have worked on

Two moves away from legacy SQL systems.

1An on-premises SQL warehouse moved to Data Lake and Databricks
2Stored procedures were rewritten as PySpark
3Processing time fell from 2 hr 50 min to about 5 min
4A manufacturing group's legacy SQL data moved to Synapse and Fabric

Stored procedures became in-memory PySpark, and the run time followed.
